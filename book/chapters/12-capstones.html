Capstone Projects

Each chapter until now gave you an architecture and asked you to complete it. This chapter gives you five specifications and no solutions. There is a gap between "I finished the tutorial" and "I can build this". These projects exist to close exactly that gap.

How to choose, and how to scope

Select one project and finish it correctly. A completed translator teaches more than five prototypes that are half built. The last 20% is evaluation, error analysis and the honest write-up. That is where you learn the things that you will remember.

Each project below has a minimum viable version and a set of extensions. Make the minimum work from start to end before you touch an extension. This is not only good discipline. A working baseline is the only thing that tells you whether an extension helped.

Key concept — scoping

These projects usually do not fail because they are difficult. They fail because someone builds three components, never connects them, and has nothing to show. First, build the ugliest possible pipeline from start to end: hard-coded hyperparameters, a very small subset of the data and no evaluation. Get one bad output from the far end. Then improve it.

Here is a specific version for any model that generates. Get one sequence out, however bad, before you tune anything. Generation bugs hide completely behind a good loss curve: the teacher-forcing shift, an <EOS> that never comes, an off-by-one error in position indexing. If you find them after a long training run, it is very discouraging.

Project 1 — A translator

Builds on 06, 08, 09.

Beat the Chapter 09 baseline on English→French. Be able to say why with a number, not an impression. The minimum is an encoder–decoder transformer trained with teacher forcing, greedy decoding, and a corpus BLEU score on a held-out set.

Here are the most valuable extensions, approximately in sequence:

  • Beam search. It is worth 1–3 BLEU. Remember length normalization, or the model will prefer short outputs.
  • Subword tokenization. This attacks the real problem: a word-level vocabulary gives many <UNK>s on French morphology.
  • Label smoothing at 0.1. It is standard in translation for a reason.
  • Length bucketing from Chapter 03. It gives free speed.

Expect overfitting, because the filtered corpus has fewer than 10,000 pairs. Thus use best-checkpoint restore from the first run. Do not wait to add it after you see the problem.

Project 2 — A text generator

Builds on 04, 10.

Train a language model on an author that you select, and generate text in a style that people can recognize as theirs. The minimum is a decoder-only transformer, a recorded validation perplexity, and four sampling configurations compared next to each other.

The first real judgment call is characters or words. The notebook gives you a survey of the corpus to help you decide. Below approximately 200k characters, word-level vocabularies overfit almost immediately. Above approximately 1M, word-level trains much faster, because each token carries more information.

Evaluating a generator honestly Perplexity measures next-token prediction, not quality. A model can have excellent perplexity and generate boring text. The two are most different at exactly the settings that you would want to ship. Use three signals together: perplexity for calibration, distinct_n for repetition, and your own reading of twenty samples. The third is not optional, and you cannot automate it away.

Project 3 — A domain-adapted assistant

Builds on 10, 11.

Train one base model. Then make several specialized variants as LoRA adapters, and show hot-swapping. Load the base one time, attach adapter A, generate, detach it, and attach adapter B. This is how adapter-based deployment really works. When you build it one time, the full approach becomes concrete in a way that reading cannot give you.

One design decision is more important than the others: train the base on a corpus outside all three target domains. If the base trains on the same data that you adapt to, the experiment cannot show what the adapters add. The base already knows each domain. If it also saw your test text, each perplexity looks better than it really is. The notebook suggests a neutral corpus for exactly this reason.

The most interesting extension is the forgetting matrix: the perplexity of each adapter on each domain, also the domains that it did not train on. It shows catastrophic forgetting as a number, not as a warning. It is also the clearest reason to keep adapters separate and not merge them.

The question worth sitting with In Chapter 11, an adapter is approximately 100 KB, and the base is 3.3 MB. A full fine-tune stores a full model for each task, but LoRA stores almost nothing. So what is really in that 100 KB, and why is it enough? The honest answer connects back to the rank sweep of Chapter 11. Almost all of what the model knows is already in the base. Adaptation is a small, low-rank adjustment of capabilities that it already has.

Project 4 — An architecture bake-off

Builds on 03–08.

Compare the bag-of-embeddings baseline, an LSTM and a transformer on one task. Make the conditions fair enough that the result means something. This project is not the most difficult technically. It needs the most rigor, and that is the point.

Two requirements make it worth doing:

  • Matched parameter counts, within approximately 10%. If they do not match, you compare capacity, not architecture. The notebook gives a binary-search helper for exactly this.
  • Three random seeds, with the standard deviation reported.
Why three seeds is the minimum On 2,000 movie reviews, the seed alone frequently changes accuracy by ±2%. If architecture A beats B by 1% on one run, you measured noise and reported it as a result. This failure is not hypothetical. It is the most common error in informal ML comparisons, and it costs almost nothing to prevent.

The most useful extension is the learning-curve study. Train each architecture again at 10%, 25%, 50% and 100% of the training data. The ranking will probably change with dataset size. Try to understand why. The transformer has the weakest inductive bias, so it needs the most data. The baseline does not model order at all. That is a very strong prior, and it stops hurting only when there is enough data to learn order from. This study connects Chapter 03 and Chapter 08 better than either chapter does alone.

Project 5 — Reproduce a paper

Builds on everything, plus the reading guide.

This project builds most directly the skill that the others only approach. Take a published result, implement it again, and write what you found. It is also the clearest thing that you can show to someone who evaluates your work. It shows three skills at the same time: reading, implementing and honest reporting.

Select something small and self-contained that this course has prepared you for. Here are good candidates, approximately in sequence of effort:

PaperClaim to reproduceWhy it is tractable here
Iyyer et al. (2015), deep unordered compositionaveraged embeddings plus depth rival far more complex models on sentimentCh. 03 is the shallow version of exactly this
Holtzman et al. (2019), nucleus samplingtop-p beats beam search on repetition metricsyour Ch. 10 model plus distinct_n
LoRA (2021), §7.2low rank suffices; rank beyond a point adds littlethe rank sweep in Ch. 11, extended
Xiong et al. (2020), pre-normpost-norm needs warmup; pre-norm does notone flag in your Ch. 08/10 block, two training runs
Press & Wolf (2017), weight tyingtying improves perplexity, not just sizeone line in your Ch. 10 GPT

The method

  1. State the claim as a number before you start. You can test "pre-norm trains stably at a learning rate where post-norm diverges". You cannot test "pre-norm is better".
  2. Implement from the paper, not from someone's code. If you cannot continue, that is the interesting part. Write exactly which detail the paper did not specify. Each paper has several.
  3. Reproduce at your scale, not theirs. You test whether the mechanism works. You do not try to match a number from a thousand GPUs. Say this explicitly in the write-up.
  4. Run three seeds, and report the spread. Half of the failed reproductions are really seed variance.
  5. Write it in two pages: the claim, your setup, the result with error bars, where you did something different from the paper, and what you could not test.
Key concept — the most valuable outcome is not "it worked"

A reproduction that confirms the claim is satisfactory. Sometimes a reproduction finds that the claim is true only under conditions that the paper did not state. That result is much more valuable, and it is common. Examples are sensitivity to the learning rate, to the initialization, to the dataset size, or to a preprocessing detail in an appendix.

That is a real contribution. You can publish it as a blog post or a workshop note. It is exactly the type of work that shows judgment, not only output. Write it carefully and generously. The goal is to inform, not to win a point against the authors.

After this

The course ends here. But the material clearly continues in several directions, and you are now ready for all of them.

  • Subword tokenization. Implement BPE from scratch. It is the last major component of a modern language model that this course does not build.
  • RLHF and DPO. This is how a base language model becomes an assistant. It is a different idea from everything here, and it is the natural next step after Chapter 10.
  • Retrieval augmentation. Embed a corpus, retrieve by nearest neighbor, and condition generation on what you retrieved.
  • MLX. This is the array framework of Apple, designed around unified memory. If you implement the GPT of Chapter 10 again in it, you see clearly what PyTorch hides from you.
  • The papers. Attention Is All You Need (2017), LoRA (2021) and QLoRA (2023) are all easy to read now, in a way that they were not before Chapter 00. You have implemented most of what they describe.
Say it backRecommended

Explain this to your favorite LLM, in your own words: what does your capstone actually show, and where would a skeptical reader attack it first? Then ask it to correct you. Ask it to question each part where you were vague, not only the parts where you were wrong, because the gaps are in the vague parts.

What a good answer covers — open this afterwards, not before
  1. What the model maps to what, in shapes: the inputs, the outputs, and what one training example really is.
  2. Why you used that loss for that output type, in one sentence, with the distribution that it assumes.
  3. What the baseline is, and what it scores. Not "a simple model", but a number, from a baseline that you tuned at least a little.
  4. What result would have proved your claim false. State it before you look at whether it did.
  5. What you cannot conclude from what you ran: the confound that you did not control, the seed variance that you did not measure, the split that you used again.

Most common miss. Each version of this that skips the last point. A project that cannot name its own limitation is not modest. It is not finished. And a skeptical reader will find that limitation first.
