Probability, Likelihood, and Loss

Before a network can learn anything, someone has to tell it what "wrong" means. That number — the loss — is not an arbitrary choice. Almost every loss function in deep learning falls out of one principle: assume your model defines a probability distribution, then maximise the probability it assigns to the data you actually saw.

It is tempting to start with architectures. Resist it. Architectures are interchangeable in a way that objectives are not: you can swap a CNN for a transformer and still be solving the same problem, but change the loss and you have changed the problem. Every later chapter in this book trains something, and every one of them minimises a quantity introduced here.

Models output beliefs, not answers

Start with a classifier that reads a film review and decides whether it is positive. The naive framing is that the model outputs an answer: positive or negative. That framing is a dead end, for a reason that has nothing to do with neural networks: a discrete answer cannot be improved by a small amount.

Suppose the model says negative and the truth is positive. How wrong was it? There is one bit of information available — right or wrong — and no way to nudge the model slightly less wrong. Gradient descent, which we meet properly in Chapter 01, needs a continuous surface to roll down. A step function has a derivative of zero everywhere it is defined, which is another way of saying it offers no advice about which direction to move.

So the model does not output an answer. It outputs a belief: a number $\hat{y} \in (0,1)$ read as "the probability this review is positive". Now "wrong" has degrees. A model that says 0.51 and a model that says 0.99 have made the same decision but are not equally wrong when the truth is 0. That difference is exactly the signal training runs on.

Key concept A neural network is a parameterised family of probability distributions. Training picks the member of that family under which your observed data is most probable. Everything else — losses, gradients, softmax, the lot — is machinery for carrying out that one sentence.

The three distributions you actually need

Deep learning uses a handful of distributions over and over. Three cover nearly everything in this book.

Bernoulli — one yes/no outcome

A single binary event with probability $p$ of coming up 1. If $y \in \{0, 1\}$ then

$$P(y \mid p) = p^{y}\,(1-p)^{1-y}$$

That exponent trick is worth pausing on, because it recurs constantly. It is not deep mathematics; it is a way to write an if statement as an expression. When $y = 1$ the formula reads $p^1 (1-p)^0 = p$. When $y = 0$ it reads $p^0 (1-p)^1 = 1-p$. One expression, both branches, and — crucially — differentiable in $p$.

Categorical — one outcome from $K$ classes

The generalisation to $K$ mutually exclusive classes with probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_K$ summing to 1. Writing $y$ as a one-hot vector,

$$P(y \mid p) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} p_k^{\,y_k}$$

Same trick: every factor with $y_k = 0$ becomes $p_k^0 = 1$ and drops out, leaving just the probability assigned to the true class. This is the distribution behind sentiment classification in Chapter 03 and behind every single token a language model predicts in Chapter 10.

Gaussian — a real-valued measurement with noise

$$P(y \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

Used whenever the target is a continuous number rather than a label — the sine-wave regression in Chapter 05, for instance. The model predicts the mean $\mu$; the spread $\sigma$ is usually assumed fixed and, as we are about to see, quietly disappears from the loss.

Likelihood: reading a distribution backwards

Here is the pivot the whole chapter turns on. A probability distribution $P(y \mid \theta)$ is normally read forwards: parameters $\theta$ are known, and you ask how probable various outcomes $y$ are. Training reverses this. The data $y$ is the thing you know — you observed it — and the parameters are unknown.

The same expression, read with $y$ fixed and $\theta$ varying, is called the likelihood of $\theta$. It is not a probability distribution over $\theta$; it does not integrate to 1 and is not trying to. It is a score: how well does this setting of the parameters explain what I saw?

For a dataset of $N$ independent examples, the likelihood of the whole dataset is the product of the individual likelihoods:

$$\mathcal{l}(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} P(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)$$

Maximum likelihood estimation is the principle that you should choose the $\theta$ maximising this product. It is the single most productive idea in the chapter.

Why we take the logarithm

That product is unusable as written, for two independent reasons.

The first is numerical, and it is not subtle. Take a modest batch of 64 examples where the model assigns probability 0.1 to each correct answer. The product is $10^{-64}$. The smallest positive number a 32-bit float can represent is about $10^{-38}$. Your likelihood is not small — it is zero, exactly, and so is its gradient. A language model over a 50,000-token vocabulary predicting a 1,000-token document underflows this way within the first dozen tokens.

The second is structural: derivatives of products are miserable (the product rule compounds across $N$ terms), whereas derivatives of sums are trivial — each term differentiates independently.

The logarithm fixes both at once. It is strictly increasing, so whatever maximises $\mathcal{l}$ also maximises $\log \mathcal{l}$ — the answer is unchanged. And it turns products into sums:

$$\log \mathcal{l}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)$$

Finally, by convention we minimise rather than maximise, so we flip the sign and divide by $N$ to keep the number comparable across batch sizes. What remains is the negative log-likelihood, and it is the loss function:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)$$
Key concept Every loss in this book is that formula with a different distribution substituted in. Bernoulli gives you binary cross-entropy. Categorical gives you cross-entropy. Gaussian gives you mean squared error. They are not three unrelated recipes — they are one recipe with three ingredients.

Deriving the losses you already use

Bernoulli → binary cross-entropy

Substitute the Bernoulli likelihood, with the model's output $\hat{y}$ playing the role of $p$:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \log\left[\hat{y}_i^{\,y_i}(1-\hat{y}_i)^{1-y_i}\right] = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \Big[ y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i) \Big]$$

That is BCE, exactly as it appears in nn.BCELoss and in the XOR network of Chapter 01. Note what the exponent trick bought us: for any given example one of the two terms is multiplied by zero, so the loss is always just $-\log(\text{probability assigned to the truth})$.

Categorical → cross-entropy

The same substitution with the categorical likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \sum_{k=1}^{K} y_{ik} \log \hat{y}_{ik} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \log \hat{y}_{i,c_i}$$

where $c_i$ is the index of the correct class. The double sum collapses because $y$ is one-hot. This is what nn.CrossEntropyLoss computes, and it is why that function takes integer class indices rather than one-hot vectors — the one-hot vector was only ever a device for selecting one term.

The widget below is the shape of that single term. Everything about how classifiers train is visible in it.

Two features matter. Near $p = 1$ the curve is flat: a model that is already right gets almost no gradient, so training stops pushing on examples it has mastered and spends its capacity elsewhere. Near $p = 0$ the curve is unbounded: being confidently wrong is punished without limit. That asymmetry is deliberate and is what makes cross-entropy train classifiers so much better than squared error does.

Gaussian → mean squared error

Substitute the Gaussian density and expand the logarithm:

$$-\log P(y \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$$

The second term does not involve $\mu$, so it has zero gradient with respect to the model's parameters and cannot affect where the minimum is. Drop it. Hold $\sigma$ fixed and it is a constant scale factor, absorbed into the learning rate. What survives is

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

So MSE is not a neutral, assumption-free choice of "how far off am I". It is the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian with constant variance. When you minimise squared error you are asserting that your targets are the truth plus symmetric, constant-width, bell-shaped noise — and when that assumption is wrong, MSE misbehaves in exactly the ways the assumption predicts.

Why MSE fails on classification

This is the point where derivation pays off in practice. Suppose you use MSE with a sigmoid output. The loss is $(\hat{y} - y)^2$ with $\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$, so by the chain rule

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = 2(\hat{y} - y)\,\sigma'(z)$$

Now consider the worst case: the truth is 1 and the model outputs 0.0001, meaning $z$ is around $-9$. You want an enormous corrective gradient. But $\sigma'(-9) \approx 0.0001$, so the gradient is multiplied by essentially zero. The more confidently wrong the model is, the less it learns. Training stalls precisely where it needs to move fastest.

Cross-entropy is built so this cannot happen — as the next section shows, the $\sigma'$ factor cancels exactly.

The same reasoning explains the regression losses. MSE's gradient grows without bound with the error, so a single mislabelled point with error 50 contributes a gradient of 100 and dominates the batch. MAE ($|e|$, the Laplace-distribution NLL) has constant gradient magnitude 1 and simply ignores how bad an outlier is. Huber splits the difference: quadratic near zero for smooth convergence, linear in the tails for robustness. Tick show gradients above to see the three derivative curves directly — the loss curves are pretty, but the gradients are what training actually feels.

Softmax, and the gradient that makes it all work

A network's final layer produces $K$ unbounded real numbers, called logits. Turning them into a distribution needs a function that is positive everywhere, sums to one, and is differentiable. Softmax:

$$\hat{y}_k = \operatorname{softmax}(z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}$$

Exponentiating forces positivity; dividing by the sum forces normalisation. The name is a little misleading — it is not a soft version of max but a soft version of argmax, returning a peaked distribution rather than an index.

Two properties are worth internalising. First, softmax is invariant to adding a constant: $\operatorname{softmax}(z + c) = \operatorname{softmax}(z)$, because $e^{c}$ factors out of numerator and denominator alike. Only differences between logits carry meaning. Second — and this is the practical consequence — you can subtract $\max_j z_j$ from every logit before exponentiating and change nothing about the answer while guaranteeing the largest exponent is $e^0 = 1$. Without that trick, a logit of 800 overflows to inf and the whole distribution becomes nan. Every serious implementation, PyTorch's included, does this.

The cancellation

Now combine softmax with cross-entropy and differentiate with respect to the logits. Write $\mathcal{L} = -\log \hat{y}_c$ for true class $c$. The derivative splits into two cases, which is where the algebra gets fiddly — but the result is one of the cleanest facts in the subject:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_k} = \hat{y}_k - y_k\;}$$

The gradient on each logit is simply predicted probability minus true probability. No $\sigma'$ factor, no saturation, no vanishing. If the model says 0.9 for a class that should be 0, the gradient is 0.9 — large, and pointing the right way. If it says 0.9 for a class that should be 1, the gradient is $-0.1$ — small, because little correction is needed.

Key concept Softmax and cross-entropy are designed as a pair. Their derivatives cancel to leave $\hat{y} - y$: the error itself, unattenuated. This is why classification networks train reliably, and it is the single most important reason to compute them together rather than separately. In PyTorch that means passing raw logits to nn.CrossEntropyLoss and not applying softmax yourself — the fused implementation is both numerically stabler and faster. The same applies to BCEWithLogitsLoss versus Sigmoid + BCELoss.
The derivation, in full

Let $S = \sum_j e^{z_j}$, so $\hat{y}_k = e^{z_k}/S$. First the diagonal case $k = c$:

$$\frac{\partial \hat{y}_c}{\partial z_c} = \frac{e^{z_c} S - e^{z_c} e^{z_c}}{S^2} = \hat{y}_c(1 - \hat{y}_c)$$

and the off-diagonal case $k \ne c$, where the numerator does not depend on $z_k$:

$$\frac{\partial \hat{y}_c}{\partial z_k} = \frac{0 \cdot S - e^{z_c}e^{z_k}}{S^2} = -\hat{y}_c \hat{y}_k$$

Now with $\mathcal{L} = -\log \hat{y}_c$ we have $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \hat{y}_c = -1/\hat{y}_c$, so for $k = c$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_c} = -\frac{1}{\hat{y}_c} \cdot \hat{y}_c(1-\hat{y}_c) = \hat{y}_c - 1$$

and for $k \ne c$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_k} = -\frac{1}{\hat{y}_c} \cdot (-\hat{y}_c\hat{y}_k) = \hat{y}_k$$

Since $y_c = 1$ and $y_k = 0$, both cases are the single expression $\hat{y}_k - y_k$.

The information-theoretic reading

There is a second derivation of cross-entropy that arrives at the same formula from a completely different direction, and it explains the name.

Define the surprisal of an event with probability $p$ as $-\log p$. It is the only function (up to scale) that is zero for certain events, grows as events become rarer, and is additive over independent events — learning two independent facts should cost the sum of their individual surprises. Measured in base 2 it is literally the number of bits an optimal code would spend encoding that event.

Entropy is the expected surprisal under the true distribution — the average cost of encoding outcomes when you know the true odds:

$$H(p) = -\sum_k p_k \log p_k$$

Cross-entropy is the average cost when you encode using beliefs $q$ while reality actually follows $p$:

$$H(p, q) = -\sum_k p_k \log q_k$$

And the gap between them is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, the pure penalty for being wrong:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \parallel q) = H(p,q) - H(p) = \sum_k p_k \log \frac{p_k}{q_k} \;\ge\; 0$$

Drag $q$ away from $p$ and watch the gap open. It is zero only when $q = p$ exactly, and it is asymmetric: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \parallel q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \parallel p)$, which is why it is a divergence and not a distance.

Here is the punchline. $H(p)$ depends only on the data, not on your model — it is the irreducible noise in the problem, and no model can go below it. So

$$\arg\min_\theta H(p, q_\theta) = \arg\min_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \parallel q_\theta)$$

Minimising cross-entropy is minimising divergence from the truth. The maximum-likelihood story and the information-theory story are the same story told twice.

Where this shows up A language model's perplexity is just $e^{\mathcal{L}}$ for cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}$ in nats. A perplexity of 40 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 40 words. When Chapter 10 reports a loss of 1.8, that is a perplexity of about 6 — the model has narrowed the whole vocabulary down to roughly six plausible next tokens. Loss curves are hard to read; perplexity is not.

Three practical notes that cause more bugs than anything else in this table.

Pass logits, not probabilities. CrossEntropyLoss and BCEWithLogitsLoss apply the softmax or sigmoid internally using the stable log-sum-exp formulation. Applying it yourself first is a silent correctness bug — the loss still runs, the numbers still go down, and the model trains worse than it should.

Ignore your padding. Sequence models compute a loss at every position, including positions that are only <PAD>. Passing ignore_index=PAD_IDX excludes them. Without it the model spends a large fraction of its capacity learning to predict padding, which it will do extremely well and which is worth nothing. Both Chapter 09 and Chapter 10 depend on this.

Class imbalance is a likelihood problem. If 95% of your labels are negative, the maximum-likelihood solution really is to always predict negative — the loss is not malfunctioning, it is answering the question you asked. Reweighting with pos_weight or weight= changes the question.

By hand — exercise 00.1

A binary classifier produces these three predictions. Compute the binary cross-entropy loss of the batch, using $\ln$ (natural log) throughout.

example true y predicted ŷ 1 1 0.9 2 0 0.2 3 1 0.4
  1. Write the per-example loss $-[y\log\hat{y} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})]$ for each row, simplifying away the term that is multiplied by zero.
  2. Evaluate each, using $\ln 0.9 \approx -0.105$, $\ln 0.8 \approx -0.223$, $\ln 0.4 \approx -0.916$.
  3. Average the three to get the batch loss.
  4. Which example contributes most, and is that the one you would have called "most wrong" by eye?
Worked solution

1 & 2. For $y=1$ only the first term survives, giving $-\ln\hat{y}$; for $y=0$ only the second, giving $-\ln(1-\hat{y})$.

example 1: y=1 → −ln(0.9) = 0.105 example 2: y=0 → −ln(1 − 0.2) = −ln(0.8) = 0.223 example 3: y=1 → −ln(0.4) = 0.916

3. Batch loss $= (0.105 + 0.223 + 0.916)/3 = 1.244/3 = \mathbf{0.415}$.

4. Example 3, at 0.916, contributes 74% of the total. By eye it is the only one on the wrong side of 0.5, so yes — but notice how much more it contributes. Example 2 is 0.2 away from perfect and costs 0.223; example 3 is 0.6 away and costs 0.916, four times as much for three times the error. That superlinearity is the log at work, and it is why one badly-classified example can dominate a batch's gradient.

By hand — exercise 00.2

A 3-class classifier outputs the logits below for a single example whose true class is class 1 (zero-indexed: the middle one).

z = [ 2.0, 1.0, 0.0 ] true class c = 1 useful values: e^0 = 1.000 e^1 = 2.718 e^2 = 7.389
  1. Apply the max-subtraction trick: subtract $\max_j z_j$ from every logit. Confirm for yourself that this cannot change the answer.
  2. Compute $\operatorname{softmax}(z)$ to three decimal places.
  3. Compute the cross-entropy loss $-\log \hat{y}_c$.
  4. Compute the gradient $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial z_k$ for all three logits, using $\hat{y} - y$. Check that they sum to zero, and explain why they must.
Worked solution

1. Subtracting 2.0 gives $z' = [0, -1, -2]$. Softmax is invariant because $e^{z_k - m} / \sum_j e^{z_j - m} = (e^{-m}e^{z_k}) / (e^{-m}\sum_j e^{z_j})$ — the $e^{-m}$ cancels. Working with the original logits is fine here since they are small; the trick matters when logits reach the hundreds.

2. $\sum_j e^{z_j} = 7.389 + 2.718 + 1.000 = 11.107$.

ŷ₀ = 7.389 / 11.107 = 0.665 ŷ₁ = 2.718 / 11.107 = 0.245 ŷ₂ = 1.000 / 11.107 = 0.090 (sums to 1.000 ✓)

3. $\mathcal{L} = -\ln(0.245) = \mathbf{1.406}$ nats. For scale: a uniform guess over 3 classes would cost $-\ln(1/3) = 1.099$, so this model is worse than guessing on this example — it put most of its mass on class 0.

4. With one-hot $y = [0, 1, 0]$:

∂L/∂z₀ = 0.665 − 0 = +0.665 push logit 0 DOWN ∂L/∂z₁ = 0.245 − 1 = −0.755 push logit 1 UP ∂L/∂z₂ = 0.090 − 0 = +0.090 push logit 2 down slightly

They sum to $0.665 - 0.755 + 0.090 = 0$. They must, because softmax outputs are constrained to sum to 1: adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing, so the gradient can have no component in that direction. Only the relative arrangement of logits is learnable — the same invariance noted above, now visible in the gradients.

By hand — exercise 00.3

A weather model claims rain with probability $q = 0.25$ every day. In reality it rains on half of all days, $p = 0.5$. Work in bits ($\log_2$), with $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

  1. Compute $H(p)$, the entropy of the true weather.
  2. Compute $H(p, q)$, what the model actually pays.
  3. Compute $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \parallel q)$, and say in words what the number means.
  4. Now reverse them: compute $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \parallel p)$. Why is it different?
Worked solution

1. $H(p) = -(0.5\log_2 0.5 + 0.5 \log_2 0.5) = 1$ bit — a fair coin, the maximum possible uncertainty for a binary event.

2. $H(p,q) = -(0.5\log_2 0.25 + 0.5\log_2 0.75)$. Now $\log_2 0.25 = -2$ and $\log_2 0.75 = \log_2 3 - 2 \approx -0.415$, so $H(p,q) = -(0.5 \times -2 + 0.5 \times -0.415) = 1 + 0.208 = \mathbf{1.208}$ bits.

3. $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\parallel q) = 1.208 - 1 = \mathbf{0.208}$ bits. In words: encoding real weather with this model's beliefs costs about 0.21 extra bits per day. Over a year that is roughly 76 wasted bits — the concrete price of a wrong model, in the currency of message length.

4. $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \parallel p) = \sum_k q_k \log_2 (q_k/p_k) = 0.25\log_2(0.5) + 0.75\log_2(1.5) = -0.25 + 0.75(0.585) = \mathbf{0.189}$ bits. Different, because the two are weighted by different distributions: the first averages the discrepancy over how often reality produces each outcome, the second over how often the model expects each outcome. KL asks "how surprised will I be", and who is doing the expecting changes the answer. This asymmetry is not a defect — it is why forward and reverse KL produce famously different behaviours (mass-covering versus mode-seeking) when used as training objectives.
