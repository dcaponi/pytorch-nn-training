Recurrence and Its Limits

When you average a review, you discard its word order completely. A recurrent network keeps the order. It reads one token at a time and carries a running summary forward. This idea is strictly more powerful than the baseline of Chapter 03. But it fails, and you must understand the reason in detail. The fix for that failure shapes all the chapters after it.

The chapter has two parts. In Part 1, an RNN reads the word vectors of Chapter 03 and classifies the same reviews: one label for each review. In Part 2, the same kind of RNN learns to predict the next character of a book: a target at every step.

The recurrence

An RNN processes a sequence one element at a time. It keeps a hidden state $h_t$, which must summarize everything up to position $t$:

$$h_t = \tanh\big(W_{xh}x_t + W_{hh}h_{t-1} + b_h\big) \qquad\qquad y_t = W_{hy}h_t + b_y$$

The important feature is that $W_{xh}$, $W_{hh}$ and $W_{hy}$ do not depend on $t$. The model uses one set of matrices at each step. Thus it can process sequences of any length. There is no parameter for each position, so the model cannot run out of them. A sequence two times as long costs two times the compute, not two times the weights.

Key concept The hidden state is a vector with a fixed size, for example 256 numbers. It must compress the full history. Suppose that the model needs something from token 1 at token 500. That information must stay alive through 499 rounds of multiplication, squashing and mixing with new input. That fixed-width bottleneck is the source of both the elegance of the RNN and its failure.
Example — what the bottleneck costs
"The keys that I left on the kitchen table this morning are ___" To choose "there" over "here", or to get subject-verb agreement right ("keys ... are", not "keys ... is"), the model needs "keys" at step 12. A 256-unit hidden state must therefore hold, simultaneously: - the subject and its number (plural) - the location already mentioned - the tense - the syntactic state (inside a relative clause) - everything else it might still need ...in 256 numbers, rewritten from scratch at every one of 12 steps.

Compare a transformer. When it needs "keys", it looks back at "keys" directly. The RNN must predict at step 2 that "keys" will be important later. Then it must protect "keys" through ten rounds of overwriting. That learning problem is much more difficult than it first seems.

Part 1 · An RNN reads the vectors of Chapter 03

Chapter 03 changed each review into vectors, one for each word, and then averaged them. The average discards the order of the words. Part 1 changes one thing: the average becomes an RNN. The reviews, the split, the vocabulary, the 600-word limit and the vectors stay the same. Here is the full change: 

e = self.embedding(ids)              # (B, 600, 64): Chapter 03's vectors, kept fixed
out, _ = self.rnn(e)                 # (B, 600, 64): h_t at every position
last = (ids != PAD).sum(1) - 1       # where each review really ends
h = out[torch.arange(B), last]       # (B, 64): the state after the last word
return self.fc(h)                    # one logit for each review

The embedding table comes from the trained Chapter 03 model, and it stays fixed. Thus each word arrives with what Chapter 03 learned about it. Chapter 03's vectors learned mainly one thing: which side of the verdict a word is on. In the notebook, the nearest neighbors of "boring" are "mess", "awful", "worst" and "bad". The RNN does not have to learn the meaning of 10,000 words again. It must learn only how to combine the vectors, in order.

Use the state at the last real word The reviews are padded at the end, and 30% of them are shorter than 600 words. After its last real word, a short review continues with <PAD> vectors, which are zeros. The RNN still runs at each of those steps, and each step multiplies the state by $W_{hh}$. Thus the information about the review fades, and the state at position 600 depends mostly on how much padding there was. Take the state at the last real word. (nn.utils.rnn.pack_padded_sequence is the library's way to stop each sequence at its real length.)

The measurement. The notebook trains the RNN two ways and compares it with Chapter 03:

model test accuracy RNN, vectors learned from zero 53–55% (2 runs) RNN, Chapter 03's vectors, kept fixed 68–73% (mean 70) Chapter 03: the average of the vectors 80% (79.5 to 80.5) The test set has 400 reviews, so one run moves by a point or two from the seed alone. Each line gives the range over several runs with different seeds.

Two results are important. First, the vectors matter more than anything else here. From zero, the RNN is almost perfect on its training reviews and close to a coin flip on new ones. It must learn 640,000 embedding numbers and the use of word order from only 1,600 labels, and it memorizes. With Chapter 03's vectors, the same RNN gains approximately 15 points.

Second, the RNN is still below the average, although it has the word order and the average does not. Something stops the RNN from using the words that it reads. The notebook measures what. It calculates the gradient of the loss with respect to the vector of each word: how much the label can change the use of that word. Relative to the last word:

words from the end relative gradient, trained RNN median of 5 runs range of the 5 runs 1 1 1 11 0.2 0.2 to 0.25 51 0.01 0.0003 to 0.08 101 3 × 10⁻⁶ 6 × 10⁻⁷ to 0.003 201 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ to 4 × 10⁻⁹

The values change a lot from run to run, so read the orders of magnitude. The trained RNN learns from approximately the last 50 to 100 words of each review, and from almost nothing before them. A review of 600 words often states its verdict early, or in the middle. The average reads all 600 words with the same weight, and the RNN effectively reads the last 50 to 100. The next two sections explain why the gradient fades, and Chapter 05 changes the architecture so that it does not.

Backpropagation through time

To train, unroll the recurrence into a feed-forward graph: $T$ copies of the same cell, one after the other. This is not a metaphor. The dynamic graph of PyTorch (Chapter 02) really builds it as the loop runs. Then backpropagate through the full graph.

The weights are shared. Thus the gradients from all timesteps add into the same tensors. Write $W_{hh}^{(t)}$ for the copy that step $t$ uses. Then, by the sum over paths:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{hh}} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{hh}^{(t)}}$$

This is true for both parts of this chapter. In Part 1, there is one loss, after the last word, and it reaches each copy through the steps after it. In Part 2, there is a loss at every step, and each copy also gets the losses after it.

This is the gradient accumulation of Chapter 02, used on purpose and not by accident. It also means that the memory cost of training increases with sequence length. The backward pass needs each intermediate activation at each timestep. For the optimizer, a sequence of 1,000 steps is a network of 1,000 layers.

Why gradients vanish

Here is the failure. It comes from arithmetic, not from bad luck. To send a gradient from step $t$ back to step $k$, you must go through each cell between them. Each crossing multiplies by the same two factors:

$$\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_k} = \prod_{i=k+1}^{t} \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} = \prod_{i=k+1}^{t} W_{hh}^{\top} \operatorname{diag}\big(\tanh'(z_i)\big)$$

This is a product of $t - k$ terms that are almost the same. If the typical magnitude of that term is $\lambda$, the gradient changes as $\lambda^{t-k}$. That is exponential in the distance. Also, $\tanh' \le 1$ always, and it is equal to 1 only at zero. Thus, if $W_{hh}$ is not large enough to compensate, $\lambda < 1$.

Set the multiplier to 0.85, and look at 30 steps back. The gradient is $10^{-2}$ of its original size. At 40 steps, the right edge of the plot, it is approximately $10^{-3}$. The information from early tokens is not distorted or noisy. It is absent, and you cannot tell it from zero. More training does not fix this, because there is no signal to train on.

Part 1 measured this on real reviews. In the trained RNN, the gradient from the label is approximately $0.01$ at 50 words from the end, and approximately $10^{-6}$ at 100 (the medians of five runs). Thus the RNN cannot learn to use the start of a long review, and it uses mostly the end.

Push the multiplier above 1, and the opposite occurs. The product explodes. It gives gradients of $10^{6}$ and an immediate nan. Exploding gradients are the easier problem, because clipping solves them completely: 

torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)

If the norm of the full gradient vector is more than the threshold, this scales the vector down. It keeps the direction and limits the magnitude. Put it after backward() and before step(). It is standard in each sequence model in this book. Chapter 09 and Chapter 10 both use it.

Asymmetry Clipping fixes explosion, but nothing like it fixes vanishing. You cannot multiply a gradient that is really zero back up to something useful. Vanishing gradients need a fix in the architecture. Chapter 05 gives that fix.

Part 2 · A target at every step: a character language model

Part 1 gave the RNN one label for each review. Part 2 gives it a target at every position: the next character of a book. The step is the same as in the figure at the top of this chapter, with 256 numbers in the hidden state. Only the output and the loss change: an output at every step, and one loss for each step. Chapter 10 trains a transformer on the same text, and compares the two.

The notebook trains on Alice's Adventures in Wonderland at the character level. At each position, the task is to predict the next character. This is a $K$-way classification, where $K$ is the size of the alphabet. It trains with cross-entropy, exactly as in Chapter 00. The target of each position is the next character of the text. Thus the targets are the input, moved one character to the right. The next section shows exactly what that means for training.

We use characters, not words, to make the problem simpler on purpose. The vocabulary has 72 symbols, not tens of thousands. There is no tokenization to get wrong, and there are no unknown tokens. The cost is that the model must learn spelling before it can learn anything else. Thus the first samples are random letters. Then they are words that you can say but that do not exist. Then they are real words without grammar. That progression is the most useful part of the exercise.

One training example: a prediction at every step

This is the part of an RNN that most readers misunderstand. Then they memorize the diagram and continue. Read this section slowly.

A training example is one window of text. The targets are the same window, moved one token to the right. Thus every position in the window has its own target: the token that comes next. The figure uses words, because words are easy to read. The notebook does the same thing with characters.

At step $t$, the input is the true token at position $t$. The hidden state $h_t$ summarizes everything up to position $t$. Thus the prediction of step $t$ is a prediction from the full prefix. The loss of the step is $-\ln$ of the probability that the model gave to the true next token. A window of $T$ tokens gives $T$ losses, and the loss of the window is their mean. Then one backward pass sends the gradient of that mean back through all $T$ steps, and the optimizer makes one update.

A common wrong picture

Many readers think of training as a list of separate examples, learned one after the other:

example 1: "the quick" -> "brown" example 2: "the quick brown" -> "fox" (learned after example 1)

The pairs are correct. Both are training pairs. But the mechanism is wrong in three ways:

  1. One pass, not separate examples. All the pairs of one window come from one forward pass. $h_2$ summarizes "the quick". The model uses $h_2$ to predict "brown", and step 3 uses the same $h_2$ when it reads "brown". The model calculates the prefix "the quick" one time, not one time for each pair.
  2. At the same time, not one after the other. The losses of all the pairs go into one mean, one backward pass and one update. The model does not first learn "brown" and then learn "fox". Each update changes the predictions for all the prefixes together.
  3. The input is always the true text. At step 2 in the figure, the top guess was "red". Step 3 reads "brown" anyway. During training, the inputs come from the text, never from the predictions. This is called teacher forcing. Without it, one early error would change the input of every later step.

The notebook checks the first point directly. It runs a full window through the model. Then it runs each prefix of that window alone. The two predictions for each prefix agree to within $10^{-5}$, which is rounding error. The full pass gives the prediction for every prefix at the cost of one pass.

Prefixes of every length. Inside one window of 100 characters, the prefixes have every length from 1 to 100. Thus the model learns to predict from a short context and from a long context at the same time. But each window starts from a zero hidden state, so the first positions of a window see almost nothing. In the notebook, the held-out loss is approximately 2.8 at position 1, where the model has seen one character. At the later positions it is 1.6 to 2.1. (The notebook has only 99 held-out windows, so the loss at each single position is noisy.)

Count the predictions. A window of 100 characters gives 100 predictions and 100 losses. The notebook has 15,980 windows. Thus one epoch trains on 1,598,000 predictions, not on 15,980.

Compare Part 1: one label for each sequence In Part 1, a review had one target, its label. The model used only the last hidden state, $h_T$, and the outputs of the earlier steps were calculated but not used. A review gave one loss. Here, a window of 100 characters gives 100. The RNN is the same in both setups. Only the loss is different: one loss for each step, or one loss for each sequence.

Evaluation is the training calculation, on new text, without the update. It is the only fair test of a language model. The training loss is not a test, and the notebook shows why with numbers. It holds out the next 10,000 characters of the book, and it measures the loss on them after each epoch:

epoch training loss held-out loss 3 1.33 1.60 <- the lowest held-out loss 15 0.74 2.06 30 0.64 2.23 guess each character from its frequency only: 3.17 uniform guess over 72 characters, ln 72: 4.28

The training loss decreased for all 30 epochs. The held-out loss decreased for only three epochs, and then it increased. After epoch 3, the model learns its 80,000 characters of training text, and it does not learn more English. The stride of the windows is 5, so each character is in 20 windows, and the model sees each passage 20 times in each epoch. (Two runs with different seeds agree to within 0.03.)

Thus the notebook measures the held-out loss after each epoch, and it keeps the weights of the best epoch, not the last. The model at epoch 3 is a better model of English than the model at epoch 30, although its training loss is two times larger.

The training loss is not a test The loss that the training loop prints comes from text that the model trains on, with dropout on. It measures how well the model fits its training text. Always report the held-out loss next to it. If only the training loss decreases, the model is memorizing.

Generating text, and the sampling question

After training, generation is a loop. Give the model a seed character, and get a distribution over the next character. Select one character, and give it back to the model. All the interesting behavior is in the selection.

Generation differs from training in one important way. During training, each input was the true next character. During generation, after the seed, each input is a sample that the model made. The model never saw its own errors during training. Thus one early error can take it into text that is not like its training text, and later errors become more probable. Chapter 09 calls this problem exposure bias. Most of the remaining behavior of generation comes from how the code selects each sample.

The obvious approach is to always take the argmax. It gives the worst output. It is deterministic, so the same seed always gives the same text. Also, it goes into loops almost immediately: "the the the the" is a reasonable greedy continuation. The other extreme is to sample from the full distribution. That gives some nonsense. A distribution over 60 characters has a long tail, and together the tail holds real probability.

The control between the two extremes is temperature. Apply it to the logits before the softmax:

$$p_k = \frac{\exp(z_k / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}$$
  • $T < 1$ makes the distribution sharper, toward the argmax. The output is conservative, repetitive and safe.
  • $T > 1$ makes it flatter. The output is creative and has more errors.
  • $T = 1$ samples from the real beliefs of the model.

Temperature never changes the ranking of the candidates. It changes only how much the top candidates control the result. In practice, 0.7 to 0.9 is the useful range for character models. Chapter 10 adds top-k and nucleus sampling, which cut off the tail and do not reweight it.

By hand — exercise 04.1

Unroll a very small RNN for three steps. Everything is one-dimensional, so all the matrices are scalars.

h₀ = 0.0 W_xh = 1.0 W_hh = 0.5 b = 0.0 inputs: x₁ = 1.0, x₂ = 0.0, x₃ = −1.0 tanh(1.000) = 0.762 tanh(0.381) = 0.364 tanh(−0.818) = −0.674 tanh(0.5) = 0.462
  1. Calculate $h_1$, $h_2$ and $h_3$.
  2. At step 2, the input is zero. Explain what $h_2$ represents, and where its value came from.
  3. Calculate $\partial h_3 / \partial h_2$, then $\partial h_3/\partial h_1$. Use $\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z)$.
  4. If this pattern continued, by what factor would the gradient decrease at each step? After how many steps would it be less than $10^{-3}$?
Worked solution

1. Each step is $h_t = \tanh(1.0 \cdot x_t + 0.5\,h_{t-1})$:

z₁ = 1.0(1.0) + 0.5(0.0) = 1.000 → h₁ = 0.762 z₂ = 1.0(0.0) + 0.5(0.762) = 0.381 → h₂ = 0.364 z₃ = 1.0(−1.0) + 0.5(0.364) = −0.818 → h₃ = −0.674

2. With $x_2 = 0$, the only contribution is $0.5 h_1$. Thus $h_2$ is only memory: a weaker echo of step 1 and nothing more. Note that it already decreased from 0.762 to 0.364 after one step that carried nothing. That decrease is the vanishing problem in the forward pass. The state forgets by itself, before any gradient is involved.

3. $\partial h_t/\partial h_{t-1} = \tanh'(z_t) \cdot W_{hh} = (1 - h_t^2)(0.5)$:

∂h₃/∂h₂ = (1 − 0.674²)(0.5) = (1 − 0.4543)(0.5) = 0.273 ∂h₂/∂h₁ = (1 − 0.364²)(0.5) = (1 − 0.1325)(0.5) = 0.434 ∂h₃/∂h₁ = 0.273 × 0.434 = 0.118

After only two steps back, 88% of the gradient is gone.

4. The factor for each step is at most $W_{hh} \cdot \max \tanh' = 0.5 \times 1 = 0.5$, and usually less. At 0.5 for each step, the gradient gets to $10^{-3}$ in $\log(10^{-3})/\log(0.5) \approx \mathbf{10}$ steps. That is ten characters, which is less than two words. A model like this cannot learn that a quotation mark from forty characters ago needs a closing mark. Real text has many dependencies of exactly that type.

The top character went from 64% to 87%. The last character almost disappeared.

This is much flatter. The least likely character now has a one-in-ten chance at each step.

When the model comes back to a state that it visited before, it must give the same continuation forever. As $T \to \infty$, the distribution becomes uniform. The output is random characters, and it ignores everything that the model learned. The two extremes destroy the usefulness of the model in opposite directions. That is why the interesting values are all in a narrow range near 0.7–1.0.

By hand — exercise 04.3 · what one window trains onMust do

A word-level RNN trains on the window "the cat sat on the mat", which has six words.

  1. Write the input and the target of each step. How many steps does the window have, and how many losses does it give?
  2. At step 2, the model gives "dog" the highest probability. During training, what is the input of step 3? Why?
  3. The losses of the steps are 0.9, 2.3, 0.4, 1.2 and 0.1. What is the loss of the window? How many backward passes does the training do for this window?
  4. Now you generate text from the seed "the cat". Which predictions does the code use? At step 2, the code samples "ran". What is the input of step 3?
  5. A student says: "First the model learns that 'the cat' goes to 'sat'. After that, it learns that 'the cat sat' goes to 'on'." What is correct in this sentence, and what is wrong?
Worked solution

1. Six words give five steps, because the last word has no next word:

step 1 2 3 4 5 input the cat sat on the target cat sat on the mat

Five steps, five predictions and five losses.

2. "sat". During training, each input is the true text. The prediction "dog" gets a large loss at step 2, and that loss trains the model. But step 3 still reads "sat" (teacher forcing).

3. $(0.9 + 2.3 + 0.4 + 1.2 + 0.1)/5 = 4.9/5 = \mathbf{0.98}$. There is one backward pass. It goes through all five steps, and the gradients from the five losses add into the same weight matrices.

4. The code uses only the prediction of step 2, the prediction after "cat". It discards the prediction after "the", because the next word of the seed is already known. The input of step 3 is "ran", the sample. During generation, there is no true text to read. The model reads its own output, even if "ran" is not the most probable word.

5. Correct: both are training pairs, and the model learns both. Wrong: "first" and "after that". Both pairs come from the same forward pass, and their losses go into the same mean and the same update. The model also calculates the prefix "the cat" only one time. Step 3 uses the hidden state that step 2 already made.

Feel for the numbersMust do

The full problem of a recurrent network is one number raised to a power. Learn that arithmetic well. Then the vanishing gradient is no longer a slogan. It is something that you can estimate in your head.

QuantityFloorTypicalCeilingRead it as
Per-step gradient factor $\lambda$00.5–0.99$>1$ explodesthe product over $T$ steps is what reaches the start
$\lambda = 0.9$, 50 steps—0.0052—half a percent survives
$\lambda = 0.5$, 20 steps—$9.5\times10^{-7}$—gone well inside one sentence
$\lambda = 1.1$, 50 steps—117—the other failure, and the reason for clipping
Gradient-clip norm0.11.05clips the explosion; does nothing for the vanishing
$\tanh'$01 at $z=0$1the factor is $\lambda = \|W_{hh}\| \cdot \tanh'$

Now do these on paper. Where you can, do them in your head. You can use a calculator for the logarithms. The goal is to know which number to use.

  1. At $\lambda = 0.9$, after how many steps is only 1% of the gradient left?
  2. Answer the same question at $\lambda = 0.99$. What does the comparison tell you about how sharp the cliff is?
  3. Your gradient norm prints $10^{4}$. Is the fix clipping, a lower learning rate, or both? Which one addresses the cause?
Answers

1. $\ln(0.01)/\ln(0.9) = 43.7$, so approximately 44 steps. That is approximately one long sentence.

2. $\ln(0.01)/\ln(0.99) = 458$ steps. A change of 0.09 in $\lambda$ gives ten times the range. The dependence on $T$ is exponential, but the usable range of $\lambda$ is narrow. Thus you cannot tune this away. You would need $\lambda$ to stay near 1 for hundreds of steps, and nothing in a vanilla RNN keeps it there.

3. Clipping prevents the step from destroying the weights. Thus it treats the symptom, and it treats it well. A lower learning rate makes each step smaller, also the steps that were correct. Neither one addresses the cause. The cause is in the architecture, and Chapter 05 changes it.

The habit. When something repeats $T$ times, ask what one repetition multiplies by. Then raise it to the power $T$. This answers almost each stability question in this book.

Say it backRecommended

Explain this to your favorite LLM, in your own words: why does a recurrent network forget, and why is it not something you can fix by tuning? Then ask it to correct you. Ask it to question each part where you were vague, not only the parts where you were wrong, because the gaps are in the vague parts.

What a good answer covers — open this afterwards, not before
  1. The network applies the same weight matrix at each timestep. Thus the backward pass multiplies by a similar Jacobian one time for each step. The Jacobian is the grid of all the partial derivatives of the outputs of a step with respect to its inputs. A multiplication by it moves a gradient back through one step.
  2. A product of $T$ similar factors is exponential in $T$. It vanishes if the factor is less than 1, and it explodes if the factor is more than 1.
  3. No value of the learning rate changes a factor of $\lambda^{T}$. The learning rate scales the step. It does not change the signal that gets to the step.
  4. Clipping fixes only the exploding half. You can limit a gradient that is too large, but you cannot recover a gradient that is already zero.
  5. Thus the fix must be in the architecture: a path from step to step that the network adds to, not multiplies through.

Most common miss. An answer treats vanishing and exploding as one symptom. They have the same cause and completely different fixes. If an explanation gives clipping as the answer to forgetting, it has missed the asymmetry.
