Self-Attention from Scratch

Recurrence forces information through a fixed-width state, one step at a time. Attention removes both limits at the same time. Each position reads directly from each other position, in one parallel operation. The mechanism is four lines of algebra, and this chapter derives all four.

The problem with summarizing

Look at this sentence: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired." To resolve it, the model must link back six words. An LSTM must encode "animal" into its cell state and keep it through six updates. During those updates, it competes with all the other information that the LSTM must remember. It can work, but it is indirect.

Attention makes the link directly. When the model processes it, it calculates a relevance score against each other position. Then it builds a representation weighted by those scores. The path from it to animal is one step long. The six words between them do not change that, and six hundred words would not change it.

Key concept In an RNN, the path length between two positions is the distance between them. Thus gradients decay with distance. In self-attention, each path length is 1. That one property is the reason that transformers replaced recurrent models. Remember it when the notation below becomes dense.
Example — the same dependency, two architectures
"The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired." ^^^^^^ ^^ position 1 position 7 RNN : information travels 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6 → 7 the gradient is multiplied by ~6 Jacobians on the way back (one matrix of partial derivatives per step — chapter 00). At a typical factor of 0.5 per step: 0.5⁶ ≈ 0.016 — 98% of the signal is gone before it arrives. Attention : position 7 computes q₇ · k₁ directly. One dot product. One step. No attenuation. The distance between them appears nowhere in the computation.

Now make the sentence longer, with 60 words between "animal" and "it". The RNN factor becomes $0.5^{60} \approx 10^{-18}$, which you cannot tell from zero. The attention calculation is exactly the same. Only the size of the score matrix changes. That is the full trade: attention pays $O(L^2)$ compute to get a path length of $O(1)$.

Queries, keys, and values

Think of attention as a soft dictionary lookup. Each position makes three vectors from its embedding, with three learned linear projections:

VectorProduced byRole
Query $q_i$$x_iW^Q$what position $i$ is looking for
Key $k_j$$x_jW^K$what position $j$ offers as a label
Value $v_j$$x_jW^V$what position $j$ actually contributes

A hard dictionary finds one key that matches exactly, and returns its value. Attention compares the query with all the keys. It changes the similarities into weights, and it returns the weighted average of all the values. It is soft and differentiable. Most important, the model learns what to look for and what to advertise, because $W^Q$, $W^K$ and $W^V$ are parameters.

The separation of keys from values is more important than it first seems. The features that let other positions find a position are not necessarily the features worth retrieving. A token can advertise "I am a noun, singular, animate" in its key, and carry completely different content in its value.

Scaled dot-product attention

$$\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

There are four steps, and each one has a job.

$QK^\top$: score each pair. $Q$ has shape (L, d_k) and $K^\top$ has shape (d_k, L). Thus the result is (L, L). Entry $(i,j)$ is $q_i \cdot k_j$, the raw affinity of position $i$ for position $j$. This matrix is quadratic in sequence length. That is the one real cost of attention: two times the context needs four times the compute and memory.

Divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$: keep the softmax in its useful range. See below. People skip this step, and then they regret it.

Softmax over the last dimension. Each row becomes a probability distribution. The attention of position $i$ is a normalized allocation over all positions. Each row sums to 1. The columns do not sum to 1, and they do not need to.

Multiply by $V$. (L, L) @ (L, d_v) gives (L, d_v). Each output row is the weighted average of all the value vectors. The weights are the attention of that position.

Why the square-root scaling

This is the one part of the formula that looks arbitrary but is not. Suppose that the components of $q$ and $k$ are independent, with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product is a sum of $d_k$ of these products. Thus:

$$\operatorname{Var}(q \cdot k) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k}\operatorname{Var}(q_ik_i) = d_k$$

The standard deviation is $\sqrt{d_k}$. At $d_k = 64$, that is 8, so scores are frequently in the range $\pm 20$. A softmax of scores with that spread is one-hot in practice. When the softmax saturates, its gradient is approximately zero, and the attention weights stop learning. (See Chapter 00 again: the softmax Jacobian contains $\hat{y}(1-\hat{y})$, which goes to zero at both ends.) Division by $\sqrt{d_k}$ gives unit variance again, and it keeps the distribution soft.

In the widget above, clear √dₖ scaling and move $d_k$ up to 128. The heatmap becomes a hard, almost diagonal pattern. That is a layer that has stopped learning, shown as a picture.

Multi-head attention

One attention operation gives one distribution for each position. Thus it can express only one type of relation. But it in our sentence needs two links: a syntactic link (it is the subject of was) and a semantic link (it refers to animal). One softmax must select one of them.

Multi-head attention runs $h$ attention operations in parallel on projections with fewer dimensions. Then it concatenates the results:

$$\operatorname{head}_i = \operatorname{Attention}(XW_i^Q, XW_i^K, XW_i^V)$$ $$\operatorname{MultiHead}(X) = \big[\operatorname{head}_1; \dots; \operatorname{head}_h\big]W^O$$

With $d_k = d_{\text{model}}/h$, the total compute is almost the same. You divide the same budget into independent subspaces; you do not add to it. Each head can specialize, and in trained models they clearly do. Some heads follow syntactic dependencies. Some attend to the previous token. Some attend to rare words. Many attend to nothing in particular, and you can prune them.

The implementation, and the reshape that trips everyone

def split_heads(self, x):          # (B, L, d_model)
    B, L, _ = x.shape
    return x.view(B, L, self.n_heads, self.d_k).transpose(1, 2)
    #                                                    (B, n_heads, L, d_k)

In practice, you do not run $h$ separate layers. One nn.Linear(d_model, d_model) calculates all the heads at the same time, and a reshape splits the result. The view divides the feature axis into (n_heads, d_k). The transpose moves the heads next to the batch dimension. Then the matrix multiplications that follow treat (B, n_heads) as the batch, and operate independently on each head.

On the way back, you must reverse both operations. The .contiguous() is not optional: 

out = out.transpose(1, 2).contiguous().view(B, L, d_model)

transpose returns a view with changed strides. It does not reorder the memory. view needs contiguous memory. Without .contiguous(), PyTorch gives an error, which is the good result. The bad result comes from reshape. It copies the data without a warning and succeeds. Thus, if your axes were wrong, it hides the error.

Masking

Two situations need you to block specific attention weights. First, padding positions carry no information. Second, in a decoder, the model must not see future positions. Both use the same method. Add $-\infty$ to the scores before the softmax: 

scores = scores.masked_fill(mask, float('-inf'))
weights = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1)

Because $e^{-\infty} = 0$, those entries get exactly zero weight. The other weights still sum to 1, because the softmax denominator also excludes the masked entries. If you mask after the softmax, the rows are not normalized. That bug makes results worse without a crash, and it is difficult to see.

Select causal mask in the widget, and the upper triangle becomes dark. That is the decoder of Chapter 09 and all of Chapter 10.

The all-masked row Suppose that a full row is masked, for example in a sequence that is all padding. Then each score is $-\infty$, the softmax denominator is zero, and the output is nan. On the next backward pass, the nan goes into each parameter, and the model is destroyed. Prevent empty sequences in your data pipeline. This bug is known for weeks of training before anyone sees it.
By hand — exercise 06.1

Do a complete attention calculation over three tokens with $d_k = 2$. The numbers give easy exponentials.

Q = [[1, 0], K = [[1, 0], V = [[1, 0], [0, 1], [0, 1], [0, 1], [1, 1]] [1, 1]] [1, 1]] e^0 = 1.000 e^{0.707} = 2.028 e^{1.414} = 4.113
  1. Calculate the raw score matrix $QK^\top$: all nine entries.
  2. Scale by $1/\sqrt{d_k} = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$.
  3. Apply softmax to row 1 only (the query $[1,0]$).
  4. Calculate the output for position 1: the weighted sum of the value vectors.
  5. Now do step 3 again with a causal mask, so that position 1 can attend only to itself. What is the output, and why is that result certain?
Worked solution

1. Entry $(i,j) = q_i \cdot k_j$:

k₁=[1,0] k₂=[0,1] k₃=[1,1] q₁=[1,0] 1 0 1 q₂=[0,1] 0 1 1 q₃=[1,1] 1 1 2

2. Multiply each entry by 0.707. Row 1 becomes $[0.707,\ 0,\ 0.707]$.

3. Apply the exponential: $[2.028,\ 1.000,\ 2.028]$. The sum is 5.056.

row 1 weights = [0.401, 0.198, 0.401]

Positions 1 and 3 have the same weight, and that is correct. $q_1 = [1,0]$ has dot product 1 with both $k_1 = [1,0]$ and $k_3 = [1,1]$. Position 2 is orthogonal to the query, and it gets the least.

4. The weighted sum of the value rows:

out₁ = 0.401[1,0] + 0.198[0,1] + 0.401[1,1] = [0.401 + 0 + 0.401, 0 + 0.198 + 0.401] = [0.802, 0.599]

Note that the output is not any input row. It is a real mixture. That is the purpose: the new representation of position 1 now contains information from position 3.

5. With a causal mask, the scores at $j > 1$ become $-\infty$. Thus $e^{-\infty} = 0$, and after normalization the row is $[1, 0, 0]$. The output is exactly $v_1 = [1, 0]$.

This is certain, and it is a general fact: the first position of a causal attention layer can only return its own value vector, because it has nothing else to attend to. Attention does not change its representation, however well the model is trained. That is why decoder-only models get so little from their first token. It is also part of the reason that a <SOS> token is useful there: nobody needs the output at that position.

By hand — exercise 06.2

Find the shapes and the cost. You need only multiplication.

B = 32 L = 128 d_model = 256 n_heads = 8
  1. What is $d_k$?
  2. Write the shape after each step: embedding → $W^Q$ → split_heads → $QK^\top$ → softmax → @V → merge heads → $W^O$.
  3. How many floats does the attention weight tensor hold? At 4 bytes each, how much memory is that? How much if $L$ becomes 1024?
  4. Count the parameters of the multi-head attention block (four projections, no biases). Does the count depend on $L$ or on n_heads?
Worked solution

1. $d_k = 256/8 = \mathbf{32}$.

2.

embedding (32, 128, 256) after W^Q (32, 128, 256) after split_heads (32, 8, 128, 32) Q @ Kᵀ (32, 8, 128, 128) ← the quadratic one after softmax (32, 8, 128, 128) @ V (32, 8, 128, 32) merge heads (32, 128, 256) after W^O (32, 128, 256)

3. $32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 128 = 4{,}194{,}304$ floats = 16.8 MB. That is for the attention weights of one layer, in one forward pass. The backward pass needs them, so the model must keep them.

At $L = 1024$: $32 \times 8 \times 1024^2 = 268$M floats = 1.07 GB. Eight times the sequence length needs sixty-four times the memory. Six layers of that use 6.4 GB on attention weights alone, before you count any parameter or activation. That is more than the budget of most laptops, and all of it is for one intermediate tensor. That is why each long-context method aims at exactly this tensor: sliding windows, sparse patterns and the tiling of FlashAttention.

4. There are four projections of $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$: $4 \times 256^2 = \mathbf{262{,}144}$. The count depends on neither $L$ nor n_heads. Sequence length changes the activations but not the weights. Heads divide a fixed budget; they do not add to it. More heads cost nothing in parameters and almost nothing in compute. That is why 8 or 16 is standard.

In the wild

In production, all of this chapter is one call: 

out = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)

It calculates exactly the formula that you just implemented. You can verify that with numbers, and you should, as the notebook does. But where possible, it uses a fused FlashAttention kernel. The difference is not only speed. The simple implementation stores the (B, H, L, L) score matrix in memory. The fused kernel calculates attention in tiles and never stores that matrix at all. Thus memory increases linearly in $L$, not quadratically.

That is the difference between a 4k context and a 128k context. It came from a systems idea, not a modeling idea. Note that, because it shows where progress in this field really comes from. nn.MultiheadAttention also includes the projections, and nn.TransformerEncoderLayer includes the full block from Chapter 08.

Feel for the numbersMust do

In attention, learn an instinct for two quantities. The first is the spread of the raw scores. The second decides if your model fits in memory. You can do the arithmetic for both on a whiteboard while someone proposes an architecture.

QuantityFloorTypicalCeilingRead it as
std of $q \cdot k$, unit-variance inputs—$\sqrt{d_k}$—8 at $d_k = 64$; this is the whole scaling argument

One attention weight0$1/L$ if uniform11 means the row has collapsed
Row entropy0near $\ln L$ early$\ln L$0 is one-hot; $\ln L$ is attending to everything

Now do these on paper. Where you can, do them in your head. You can use a calculator for the logarithms. The goal is to know which number to use.

  1. $d_k = 64$, and you forget the scaling. Approximately what range do the raw scores cover? What does softmax do with numbers of that size?
  2. $L = 128$ with perfectly uniform attention. What is each weight? What is the row entropy?
  3. You double the sequence length. What happens to the memory for the score matrix? What happens to the parameter count?
Answers

1. The standard deviation is 8. Thus $\pm 3\sigma$ is a range of approximately $\pm 24$. The gap between the top score and the next score is then frequently several units. $e^{\text{several}}$ makes the softmax one-hot in practice. Chapter 07 shows what that does to the gradient.

2. Each weight is $1/128 = 0.0078$, and the entropy is $\ln 128 = 4.85$ nats, which is the maximum. An untrained head is near this value. When this number decreases, you see the head decide what it is for.

3. Memory increases four times, because the matrix is $L \times L$. The parameter count does not change at all. The projections are $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}$, and they do not depend on length. Almost everyone first guesses that a longer context needs a bigger model. It needs a bigger activation budget.

The habit. Separate "how many parameters" from "how much memory while the model runs". In attention, they depend on completely different things. When people confuse them, a small model surprises them with an out-of-memory error.

Say it backRecommended

Explain this to your favorite LLM, in your own words: why are there three projections rather than one, and what would break if keys and values were the same vector? Then ask it to correct you. Ask it to question each part where you were vague, not only the parts where you were wrong, because the gaps are in the vague parts. Use each of the words query, key and value only one time. Say what the vectors do.

What a good answer covers — open this afterwards, not before
  1. The query says what a position looks for. The key says what a position offers as a label. The value says what the position really contributes.
  2. The model only compares queries and keys. They make a score, and they never occur directly in the output.
  3. The model only averages values. Values never affect who attends to whom.
  4. Thus the features that let others find a position are not necessarily the features worth retrieving. If keys were tied to values, they would have to be the same.
  5. All positions share the projections. Thus the projections are properties of the model, and the vectors that result are properties of the token.

Most common miss. An answer explains the dictionary analogy but does not say what the separation gives. If your answer would still make sense with $K = V$, it has not found the reason for three matrices and not two.
